good idea. I’ve gotten more interested in using Instagram as a way to genuinely share with people

more of a dialogue this is, the more fun it will be, i think.

WHAT IM READING/WATCHING LATELY
- The Dispossessed, Ursula Le Guin
o This book genuinely shifted how i think about property, ownership, sharing, and
giving. As I was packing to leave Alaska, i’d stop and look at some possession or
other with a new, odd awareness that there is nothing material that makes it “my
property.” Like when you repeat a word over and over until stops making sense.
Thanks to Gracie for sharing the book with me. Le Guin’s rigorous imagining of
functioning anarchist society really makes you look more radically at our own present.
- Sabbath as resistance, Walter Brueggemann
o I’ve been on a little journey reinterpreting the religion (Evangelical Christianity) i
was raised in. I heard of Brueggemann from the poet Pádraig Ó Tuama, in his talks
on “Strange Stories of the Bible” (which were also excellent). This book was the only
one the Springdale Public Library had, and in it, Brueggemann articulates sabbath as
a radical divestment from commodity consumption/production. If sabbath is restfulness, then its opposite is restlessness. Once again, the bible’s radical side
drawn out by a sharp analysis...
- History of Love, Nicole Krauss
o Very smoothly and intelligently written, only complaint was how overplayed
romantic love is.
- Greek Lessons, Han Kang
o Loved the ruminations on language, and feel the novel, while concept-driven,
successfully characterizes those populating it, something I can’t say of similar fiction
by Calvino or Tawada.
- Teaching critical thinking, hooks (in progress)
o So many good learnings. Biggest shift so far is that deference in the classroom
isn’t necessarily domination: ”To honor a teacher with reverence does not require
subordination. In a democratic society where there is so much emphasis on equality,
there is a tendency to forget that inequality does not necessarily mean domination is
taking place” (114). 🤯

Syrian films—Anchorage for Palestine is wanting to screen some in January

ONYX COFFEE REPORT
My thoughts this month about Onyx were spurred by an article about digital surveillance by
Zoë Hitzig. This quote in particular:
In her 2015 book In Defense of Secrets, the late French psychoanalyst Anne Dufourmantelle
corrects one of the implicit claims of the surveillance-as-intimacy perspective: “Transparency is not truth,” she writes.
The essay as a whole argues that big tech has hoodwinked us into seeing surveillance “as a
form of intimacy.” Being on someone’s Find My is a badge of closeness; BeReal catches us in
candid moments. However, “the sense that we are showing less filtered and more continuous
footage of our lives to our friends distracts us from the reality that we are also showing all
of it to the extraordinarily powerful tech companies that gave us the idea to do so in the
first place.”
The transparency-is-not-truth quote comes as Hitzig considers how unmediated digital access
to friends and partners’ lives can warp these relationships. Surveillance leaves no opportunity
for trust or to see each other as autonomous individuals with the right to secrets! The “true”
friend is not the one you know everything about: where they’ve been, who they’ve talked to,
etc. The “true” friend is the one right in front of you, whose depths you can never fully know.
Onyx Coffee Lab prides themselves on being transparent. One place this manifests is their
price breakdowns. They publish how much they paid for a coffee, how much went to shipping,
importers, and roasting. Another site of transparency, though, is their packaging. Each
coffee or tea box has a paragraph long description of its origin. The question I am asking is:
in the face of all this information, where is the truth? Or is this just a surveillance logic
calculated to generate yet another faux intimacy?
